01 — The Work

Turning public-health simulations into a playable experience

Two exhibits for UT Austin's visitor center. The audience runs from kids to professors, nobody reads instructions, and the hardest constraint turned out to be how far a person can reach.

03 — The Problem

How do you make complex simulation data playable?

TACC works with complex datasets that can be used to simulate real-world systems and phenomena. But the data itself isn't easy for a general audience to understand.

The challenge was to translate that complexity into something visitors could interact with and understand through play, rather than asking them to read or interpret the data directly.

Tiny Travelers

A pandemic simulation. Release a contagion into a map of Austin, use tools to slow it down, watch the city change.

It runs on the SEIR model, which simulates how infectious diseases spread through a population. People move between neighbourhoods, and the population shifts through healthy, exposed, infected and recovered as they go.

The tools you place reflect real practices anyone can follow: masks, vaccinations and social distancing.

The round, running: the Tiny Traveler moves between hubs while tools fire from the four trays.

Detective Doc

A genetic makeup and environmental habits simulation. Pick a genetic makeup, then find out which everyday habits help or hurt that particular body.

You play as a detective. Pick a patient's genetic makeup, search their home and daily surroundings, and bag anything that might explain the health problems showing up in their timeline.

Each piece of evidence reveals whether that habit is helping or harming this particular body, and the answer changes depending on the genes you started with.

It helps visitors understand that there is no single list of good and bad habits. What protects one body can put another at risk.

The reel, running: choosing a case, searching the environment, and bagging evidence against the patient's genetics.

NoteThis is the final prototype, not the final game or its animation: what a player sees, where each element sits, and the rules of a turn. It was approved and handed to the animation and development teams, who build the finished exhibit from it.

Same table, same audience, and the audience is the hard part.

Kids, students, professors and families all walk up to the same screen.

Nobody reads a tutorial.

Most give it three to five minutes, standing, with strangers crowded around them.

04 — What I Learned

One round of research. Both exhibits were built on it.

The finding everything else came from

People need to see what's happening without being told.

Games work when you can read the situation at a glance. They fail when the meaning is hidden behind text, menus or backstory. And looking clear beats being accurate: a simplified model people understand teaches more than a precise one they don't.

Where that came from

12

games studied across tower defense, simulation, sandbox and outbreak genres

6

UX points checked in each game: how state is shown, where the tools sit, how spread is visualised, tone, first-time use, and how it holds up with several people at once

5

educators at UT Austin on what keeps a mixed-age group engaged and what loses them


Five rules I built to

01

Show the state instantly. If someone has to read to find out what's happening, it's already failed.

02

Ask for as little thinking as possible. Every extra decision loses people.

03

Teach by doing. Nobody reads the tutorial. They press something and find out.

04

Never make it frightening. This is a disease exhibit in a public room with children in it.

05

Work for a crowd. Several people, no turns, no waiting.

05 — Key design moments

Six decisions

01

Splitting the table in two

Both exhibits

The problem

A 90-inch table creates unreachable zones.

To validate reachability, I created a full-scale paper prototype of the table and tested it with four people of different heights. The physical test exposed a consistent issue: the center of the table created unreachable zones for shorter users, children, and wheelchair users.

Considered, not taken

One board across the whole table

Uses the whole surface, but makes the center difficult for some players to reach.

What I chose

Two separate simulations, one per half, instead of one board across the whole table.

Everyone gets a board within reach.

Eight tool trays, four per side, so nobody needs to reach across the table.
02

Where the tools live

TINY TRAVELLERS 

The problem

Splitting the board fixed the reach, but not the tools.
The controls were still shared, leaving players reaching across the table to access them.

Considered, not taken

One shared set of controls

Someone always has to reach across the table to get to them.

Tools in a hidden menu

My research ruled this out early: in an exhibit, people tend to use what they can see.

Tools placed randomly around the table

Wherever you stand, you would not have access to the same four tools as everyone else.

What I chose

Every side of the table has access to the same four tools.

 Eight tool trays, four per side, positioned at the edges so players can reach everything without crossing the table. The experience stays consistent, no matter where you stand.
03

Keeping red and green, but never alone

TINY TRAVELLERS

The problem

Red and green are familiar signals, but colour alone isn't enough.

Red and green are commonly used to communicate status, making them intuitive for many people.

But colour blindness can make the distinction difficult. And for younger children, colour alone may not communicate what each state means.

What I chose

Keep red and green, but give every state a second signal.

Pair colour with an icon, shape, or movement so the meaning doesn't depend on colour alone.

Maintain at least 4.5:1 contrast for important UI elements.

The rule I held to: colour never carries meaning by itself.

04

How people actually touch it

TINY TRAVELLERS

The problem

Drag and drop may be intuitive, but it isn't the first interaction people try.

My initial research suggested drag and drop would feel natural for a walk-up audience.

What testing changed

People tapped first.

Without instruction, people naturally tapped the tools before trying to drag them.

That made tap the safer starting point, while keeping drag available for people who discover it.

Considered, not taken

Drag and drop only

Works once someone understands the interaction, but relies on users discovering the gesture on their own.

What I chose

Support both, tap first. Tap provides a simpler interaction for children and people with lower dexterity, while drag remains available for those who prefer it.

Tap a tool, then tap a citizen — or drag it directly onto them.

Both interactions work, so players can use whichever feels easier.
05

Ethics as a design constraint

Detective Doc

The problem

How do you use real genetic data without turning people into labels? Using ethnicity, gender, or age could communicate those patterns, but it could also make demographic categories feel like defining characteristics of an individual.

Considered, not taken

Label patients by demographic group

These labels exist in the underlying data and can help explain population-level differences. But on an individual profile, the label can overshadow the biology.

What I chose

Describe the biology, not the person.

The data stays the same. Only the labels change.

Each case is identified by the condition being investigated — blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol, insomnia, thyroid, or liver function.

 Real data. No demographic labels attached to the individual profiles.
06

Knowing what's touchable

Detective Doc

The problem

Visitors couldn't tell what was interactive. 30 objects, but only 7 were clues. Without a clear distinction, visitors tapped decoration and assumed nothing worked.

Testing showed they also needed to know their progress.

Considered, not taken

Search the whole room

Fun in a timed game, but too much searching for a short, drop-in exhibit.

 Marked hotspots + running count

Shows visitors what they can investigate and how much is left—without making them spend their limited time searching.

What I chose

Make clues visible. Make progress clear.

Magnifiers show what can be investigated; checkmarks show what’s been found. A running 3/7 count tells visitors exactly how much is left.

What can I touch? What have I found? What's left?

What I'd measure next

Whether a stranger can explain what they learned 30 seconds after walking away.

Then how long people stay, whether they play again and change their choices, and how often anyone asks a staff member what something means, which would tell me the interface failed.

08 — What I'd Do Differently

01

We tested with participants, not in the space

Every session ran with recruited participants instead of the visitor centre floor.

WhyThe board is expensive and hard to move, and testing in the centre carried ethical and technical constraints we could not clear in time.
CostEnvironment does the work here: who is watching, who is waiting, how loud the room is.
FixTesting in the space would be closer to a social experiment, and would surface findings a recruited session never will.
